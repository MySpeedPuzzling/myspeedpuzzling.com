                            First-try predictions run slow. Solvers beat the statistical prediction by a median 11% (only 30% land inside the range); repeat predictions are spot on (median 1.007, 76% inside). A plain "faster than predicted" list would say "you beat it" for almost everything. Insights compares against expected for you (prediction × your own usual ratio) until the model is recalibrated, which is worth doing anyway: it also affects the recap.

                            It is a 500-piece world. A typical player with 25+ results has 1 piece count and about 3 brands with 5+ results. So the page defaults to the main size, and every split shows its sample size and confidence. Brand differences are real for ~500 players at 10+ results, and mostly noise at 5-9 (30% of brands look 10% off at n 5-9 vs 18% at 15-29).

                            No time of day. finished_at is a date for 100% of results, and tracked_at is the logging time (same day for 73%). Weekday works; time of day would be invented. Stored predictions were only partly backfilled in the dev copy, so check production coverage first.

                            Principles

                            • You against yourself first. Every split is your time ÷ puzzle median, divided by your form at that moment. That removes puzzle difficulty and your improvement, so "Clementoni is your strength" means Clementoni, not "you solved Clementoni recently".
                            • Every section answers one question, in the player's words, with a one-sentence takeaway above the chart. The question chips at the top jump to them.
                            • Show how sure we are. Ranges, sample sizes, and "not sure yet" are first-class. Nothing is called a weak spot on fewer than ~10 results, and small samples are pulled toward your usual (shrinkage).
                            • Every chart drills down to puzzles. A row, bar or dot opens the results behind it, so a nerd can check the claim.
                            • Turn insight into a next step. Re-solve this, try this from your shelf, mark these first tries. Links into the Puzzle Picker and puzzle pages.
                            • Neutral wording. "Weak spot" and "slower than your usual", never blame. The same rules as "needs verification".
                            • The URL holds the filters, like Compare, so a view can be bookmarked and shared with yourself; members-only values are stripped server-side.

                            Pairs & teams insights

                            • Synergy per exact pair/team (puzzling_team): expected ÷ actual, with expected = c(size, first try or repeat) × difficulty ÷ Σ(1 ÷ baselineᵢ): the members working perfectly in parallel, corrected by a coordination constant learned in the batch so the typical pair/team scores 0%. Measured constants: pairs 1.21 (first try) / 1.08 (repeat), teams of 3 1.35 / 1.24, 4+ 1.41 / 1.32; spread about ±10%, and including difficulty narrows it.
                            • Reach (production copy): 4,275 players have pair/team results; 2,417 have a pair/team with 5+ results. A synergy score needs no guests, a baseline for every member at that piece count, a rated puzzle and not unboxed: 943 pairs/teams qualify, giving 1,235 players a synergy number (409 two or more). Guests are listed without a score; "together vs. alone" works for everybody.
                            • Who counts: every member equally (the member table matches the team snapshots exactly, 137,086 = 137,086); "tracked by me" only for display.
                            • Together vs. alone: how many times as fast as your solo pace you finish with each partner.
                            • Best results together, synergy over time, which puzzles suit the pair (the skill map for a pair), and "who to invite for a 1000".
                            • The same filters apply; pair/team results never mix into the solo sections (like with like, as on Compare).

                            Rating opportunity engine

                            "How many points would I gain if…" is computable exactly, because the rating is a top-100 mean of per-puzzle points: percentile × difficulty weight × decay, 75% first tries + 25% best times.

                            • Re-solve: new best = min(best, expected time); decay resets to 1 even without improvement, because the entry ages by the latest solve. Gain = 0.25 × Δ(top-100 mean of best times) × 1000.
                            • First try from your shelf: unsolved puzzles from your collections that qualify (rated, 20+ solvers): percentile of your expected time × weight, into both portfolios (0.75 + 0.25).
                            • Defend: entries leaving the 24-month window in the next 60 days, with the points they take with them.
                            • Points per hour: gain ÷ expected minutes, the number data nerds will love.
                            • Needed: the calculator keeps percentiles and points in memory only. Write a player_rating_entry row per player × puzzle × portfolio (time, percentile, weight, decay, points, in top 100) during the 15-minute run, plus per-puzzle percentile lookups (stored quantiles of FA/BT times). The what-ifs then run on read, for one player. Individual moves are worth 0.5-3 points (it is a mean of 100), so the page also shows the sum of the best 5 or 10.

                            The data warehouse: what only we can know

                            Research 2026-10-18 on the production copy (523k results, 7,598 players with results; read-only, aggregates only, no names). Full report with every table: .claude/worktrees/insights-data-lab/report.md (local). Everything here is observational; numbers that use baselines and difficulty together are partly circular and get an out-of-sample check before anything public.

                            Doubling the pieces ≈ 2.7× the time

                            Within the same player: pieces1.30 from 300→500, pieces1.43 from 500→1000 (1,529 / 1,217 players). The fastest puzzlers scale best (1.25 vs 1.47).

                            Difficulty is the same for everyone

                            A hard 500 is 1.49–1.54× an easy one in every speed band. One result lands within ≈ ±11% of its par (your baseline × the puzzle's difficulty). That makes a golf-style handicap fair across levels.

                            Power law of practice

                            Newcomers: −15% after 50 puzzles, −23% after 150 (difficulty-adjusted 500s). A fixed group active in 2024 and 2026 got 15% faster in two years (70.0 → 59.5 min).

                            Pairs and teams

                            A pair is 1.46× faster on a 500 (73% efficiency each), a team of 4 2.3× (57%); a pair on a 1000 83%. Co-puzzlers form one network: 57% connected, a median 6 steps apart, 15% of links cross a border.

                            Events and sessions

                            Events look 11% faster, but event puzzles are ~5% easier; the real boost is ≈ 2.5%. Within the same player: no break 3% faster, 5+ breaks 11% slower, the 4th puzzle of a day 6% faster (warm-up, not fatigue).

                            The second day decides

                            One result in the first 30 days: 20% still active 6–12 months later. Results on 3+ days: 66%. An event result: 89%. 15+ results in month one → 29% become members (1–4 results: 5.6%).

                            Image intelligence: one complete extraction run

                            Stored, not shown (Jan, 2026-10-18). On day one nothing from the image analysis appears on puzzle pages, search, filters or SEO. It lives in the database and feeds Insights only: skill-map rows, the puzzler type, the difficulty explainer behind the scenes. Public surfaces (tags on puzzle pages, theme filters and pages, "hard because…", predicted difficulty, look-alikes, alt text) come later, one at a time, each after a quality check of its own.

                            What makes a puzzle hard (first signal)

                            160 rated puzzles, Spearman. Easier: regular/geometric layout (−0.40), text in the picture (−0.33; −29% difficulty as a label), flat colour fields (−0.22), colourfulness (−0.21). Harder: busy fine detail (+0.24; +22%), one colour mass dominating (+0.20; +15%). "Large uniform areas" is really two traits: flat colour fields bounded by edges help, one big mass of similar colour (sky, sea) hurts. Small sample and brand house styles confound it: rerun on all ~6.3k rated puzzles before the page claims anything.

                            The split

                            M3 Max: artwork first (~24 h), then describe (~123 h). M1 Air: phase A needs nothing: OCR/barcode (~3.5 h, so describe never waits) and the full-image SigLIP embedding; phase B (requires=artwork): crop embeddings, DINOv2, segmentation on the GPU with colors/traits in CPU workers beside them. ~2.4 days, then it joins describe (~1,500 a day vs the M3 Max's ~8,000). Total ≈ 5.5 days (6.1 without the Air's help).

                            Why this way round

                            Describe is ~80% of all compute and must be one model for the whole catalogue: on the Air alone it would take 27 days. Apple Vision slows from 0.13 to 14.8 s/image when a language model saturates the same GPU, so OCR lives on the other machine. Everything the Air runs peaks at ≤ 3.5 GB.

                            What the description model is not good at

                            It writes good captions but invents values outside the allowed lists and marks difficulty traits on almost everything, so traits come from numbers, never from the language model. SigLIP zero-shot ranks well but its probabilities are uncalibrated: every key gets a threshold calibrated on labelled examples. Box fronts rarely show barcodes (4 of 300), so images will not fill missing EANs; piece counts were readable on 17 of 300 and all matched.

                            Predicting difficulty and your time from the image

                            Research 2026-10-18 on the production copy plus the ~240 labelled pictures already embedded (no new downloads). Full report: .claude/worktrees/insights-prediction-lab/report.md (local). Error = average |log(actual ÷ predicted)|, so 0.10 ≈ 10% off.

                            The biggest win needs no images. Each difficulty index divides an old time by the solver's current baseline, but players get faster, so older solves look harder (+10% for solves 1–2 years old, +24% for 3+ years). That is exactly why statistical first-try predictions run ~10% slow. Using the baseline the solver had at the time cuts the error from 0.126 to 0.092 on the same solves; rated scores drop ~8% at the median and ~46% of rated puzzles move one tier easier.

                            Pace history (Jan's idea) fixes it

                            One row per player × piece count each time their pace changes (each qualifying first try): baseline, sample size, drift, the solve that moved it. ~225k rows to backfill from all history (~30 MB, minutes), ~320 new rows a day; a daily full copy would be 6.4M rows a year. The same table gives unbiased difficulty, consistent reconstructed predictions, and the improvement chart: curve per size, "your biggest jumps" and on which puzzle (steps > 3% in 8% of solves), plateaus against the typical curve (500 pc: −7.6% after 50 first tries, −15% after 150).

                            Image vs today, same solves

                            Today: 0.126 error at 91% coverage. Corrected today: 0.092 at 91%. Image prior blended with the first solves: 0.095 at 100% coverage. Image alone: 0.161. One real solve beats the image (prior helps by 26% at 0 solves, ~4% at 1–2, nothing from 3). Images: Spearman 0.72 (pure image + pieces), 0.65 on a random test, 0.74 newer-from-older; expected R² ≈ 0.55 once ~2.5–5k labelled puzzles are embedded.

                            Piece counts are the real gap

                            500 pc = 78% of solves and 80% of its first tries get a prediction. ≤300: 27%. 1000: 16% (49% have a baseline but an unrated puzzle). 1500+ and odd sizes: ~0%. Time grows like pieces1.35; for 1000s a scaled 500 baseline beats the player's own thin one. The same artwork at 500 and 1000 keeps its difficulty (Spearman 0.88): difficulty is mostly the picture, so an image prior helps exactly the sizes with few solves.

                            Personal taste: real, small, handled

                            A player × puzzle taste term explains 1–2.5% of what is left on later solves; unshrunk it makes things worse (−9.5%). Shrunk by n/(n+100) and damped it gains 4% overall, 11% for players with 100–299 solves, never more than 0.5% worse for anyone. Improvement drift matters far more (0.160 → 0.137). So the model works without taste and adds it carefully as data grows.

                            How an estimate could look: three options (mock-up)

                            The same puzzle in three states: nobody has solved it (picture only), 2 solves (picture blended with the first solves), and 14 solves (measured, as today). The difficulty tier itself is a property of the puzzle: the research shows a hard puzzle is equally hard for every level (1.49–1.54× an easy one in every speed band), so a "personal tier" would mostly repeat the same tier. Where personal taste does show up, it becomes a line like "probably harder for you than usual", not a different tier.

                            How it is built

                            Centred on the corrected prediction (pace at the time + shrinkage + drift). Width from what we know: your own consistency (recent spread, shrunk toward everyone's with few solves), how well the puzzle is measured (solves, or the picture), first try vs repeat, piece count. Asymmetric, because slow days stretch further than fast ones. Every bucket lands within ±2 points of its promise.

                            How narrow it gets

                            Most consistent quarter of players on well-measured puzzles: 50% window 11% wide (≈ ±6%), ≈ 7 min on an hour. Least consistent quarter: 16%. Puzzles with 1–4 solves: 20%; picture or nothing: ~30% today (the image prior narrows that by ~20%, more after the backfill). 1000s: 27% until they get more solves.

                            Wording and rounding

                            "Likely 57–64 min · half of solves land inside". A hit: "Inside your likely window: only half of solves do". A miss: the side and the margin ("3 min faster than likely"), never framed as failure. Window ends round to the nearest minute under 20 min wide (5/10 min above), never outward: rounding outward turned a 50% window into a 76% one.

                            Detail sheet: the 80% range, what the width rests on, and your own hit rate so far. Recalibrated nightly from the stored predictions; a bucket drifting more than 3 points from its promise raises an alert. First-try windows need the pace-at-the-time fix first: no width works around a biased centre.

                            Realtime

                            Everything is precomputed; a page only does arithmetic in PHP (microseconds, no extra query when it rides on the difficulty join), so lists stay fast. The Macs claim new images every few minutes; 76% of first-ever solves are logged within 10 minutes of the puzzle being added, so k = 0 estimates matter for the catalogue and the next solvers rather than before the very first solve. SigLIP 2 takes ~0.4 s per image on CPU, so on-server embedding is possible but not needed. Secret puzzles get no estimate, no training row and no "looks like" entry until revealed.

                            2. Predictions from 1–4 solves and a scaled pace for thin sizes: first-try coverage 66% → 82%, biggest gain on 1000s and ≤300s.

                            Missing data and how to gather it

                            Puzzles have almost no descriptors today: no theme, colour, cut, finish, artist or year. Tags are event labels. That is the biggest gap for "what kind of puzzle suits me".

                            1. Image features, computed (recommended first)

                            OpenCV over every box image: colour entropy, dominant palette, share of large uniform areas, edge density (busyness), gradients, brightness/contrast, text regions. Deterministic, self-hosted, cheap, and we already run an OpenCV worker (tools/online-cv/). Run once over ~37k images, then on every new image. Stored as numbers, bucketed into traits for the UI.

                            2. Vision model tags (second)

                            A vision-language model for things pixels cannot express simply: theme (landscape, animals, food, collage, map, city), style (photo, painting, illustration, cartoon), dominant subject, "repeating patterns". Prefer an open model self-hosted (in line with the online-competitions rule); a hosted API is the fallback. Fixed tag vocabulary, confidence stored, a moderator can correct.

                            3. Crowd answers after a solve

                            Cut type (grid / random / ribbon / whimsy), finish (matte / glossy / linen), "pieces missing", as one optional tap on the recap. Majority vote per puzzle, brand defaults where a brand has one cut (e.g. Ravensburger grid). It also feeds karma. Players know these facts better than any model.

                            4. Better data on results

                            Nudges to mark first tries on old results, unboxed solves, solve date. Optional stopwatch milestones ("edge done", "half") for solve phases, and storing the stopwatch id on the result (it is guessed today). Optional per-result tags: at an event, livestream, new place.

                            Method notes

                            • Normalisation: ratio = your time ÷ puzzle median (solo). Your form = rolling median of first-try ratios (±75 days). Residual = ratio ÷ form; repeats are also divided by the community repeat factor so they compare with first tries.
                            • Segment effect = median residual, likely range from the spread ÷ √n, shrunk toward 0 by n ÷ (n + 8). Strength / weak spot only when the whole range is beyond ±1.5% (tune on real data).
                            • Outliers = residual beyond ±30% (median player: ~4% each side, about 5 results).
                            • Expected for you = stored prediction × your median actual/predicted ratio for that method in the filtered period.
                            • Difficulty splits will look flat for most people (the median already absorbs difficulty: only 12% of tier segments are 10% off). Word it as "you hold up on hard puzzles", not as a ranking.
                            • Privacy: owner-only page, nothing public, private players included (their own data); never shows other players' names except the existing public rankings.
